Almost every club inventory I have seen was abandoned for the same reason: it started with the spreadsheet instead of the store room. Twenty beautiful columns get set up, forty rows get filled in, and three months later nobody knows whether the rope in their hand is row 12 or row 31.

The useful order is the opposite one.

## Step 1: make every item distinguishable

Before recording anything, you need to be able to look at an item and know which one it is. If you have six identical 60-metre ropes, without a physical identifier you do not have an inventory — you have a headcount.

What works:

- **Numbered heat-shrink** on the ends of ropes and slings. Cheap, durable and it does not interfere with use.
- **Textile marking** with a pen approved for textile gear — never solvents or industrial markers, which can degrade the fibres.
- **Engraving or stickers** on metal hardware and helmets.

The code must be **short and unique**: `C-014`, not `Blue Beal 60m rope bought in 2024`. The description belongs in the record; the identifier belongs on the item.

If the club has sections, prefix by section: `CLB-C-014`, `CAN-N-007`. It will save you a reorganisation later.

## Step 2: decide what you will record, and nothing more

The temptation is to record everything. Resist it: every field you add is a field somebody has to fill in every single time, and inventories die of friction.

The minimum that actually gets used:

| Field | What it is for |
|---|---|
| Identifier | Links the physical item to the record |
| Type and model | Determines which manufacturer instructions apply |
| Date of manufacture | Starting point of service life |
| Date first put into service | The real clock starts here |
| Status | Available, on loan, under inspection, retired |
| Location | Where it lives when it is not on loan |

Everything else — colour, price, supplier — is optional and can wait.

## Step 3: work in batches, not in perfection

Do not try to do it all in one Saturday. Take one category, ropes for instance, and finish it completely: identify, record, store. Then harnesses. Then helmets.

One finished category is worth more than five half-done ones, because you can already start using the system for that category while you complete the rest.

## Step 4: whatever you do not know, mark as unknown

You will find gear with no legible date of manufacture, no label, or donated years ago with no paperwork. The temptation is to estimate. Do not.

Record **unknown** explicitly, because unknown is data: it means that item has no traceability and, for critical equipment, probably has to be retired. An invented date turns a visible problem into a hidden one.

## The four mistakes that force a restart

**Identifiers that depend on order.** If you number 1, 2, 3 as you pull items out of the box, the day you retire number 7 you will have a gap and somebody will reuse it. Use codes that are never recycled.

**Recording purchase instead of first use.** Gear bought in 2023 and first used in 2025 has two different dates, and the one that counts for service life is not always the one you think. Keep both.

**A single keeper.** If only one person knows how to use the system, the inventory lasts as long as that person stays in the role. At least two people should be able to do everything.

**Not recording retirements.** An inventory that only grows is lying. Retired gear has to appear as retired, with a date and a reason — and physically leave circulation.

## Once the inventory is alive

From here on, what matters is that the record updates itself as a side effect of what you already do: lending, receiving, inspecting. If updating the inventory is a separate chore somebody has to remember, you will be back to the abandoned spreadsheet.

That is exactly what [RocNest](/en) does: the record is a consequence of the loan and the inspection, not extra work. It is free and open source.

But even on paper, if you get the four steps above right you have a real inventory. The rest is convenience.
