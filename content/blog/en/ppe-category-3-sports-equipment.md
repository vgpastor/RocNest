There is a `CE` followed by four digits on the label of your harness. Most people read it as a generic quality stamp. It is considerably more than that, and understanding it changes how you buy gear for the club.

## The three categories

[Regulation (EU) 2016/425](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0425) classifies personal protective equipment by the risk it protects against:

- **Category I** — minimal risks. Gardening gloves, sunglasses. The manufacturer self-certifies.
- **Category II** — intermediate risks. Requires type examination by a notified body.
- **Category III** — risks that can cause **very serious consequences or death**. This is all your progression and safety gear: ropes, harnesses, helmets, carabiners, belay devices, anchors.

## What category III implies

It is the most demanding category, and the three requirements that affect you directly are:

**EU type examination.** An independent notified body has verified the design. This is not the manufacturer claiming compliance: it is an accredited third party.

**Ongoing production control.** Certifying the prototype is not enough. The manufacturer is subject to periodic surveillance, which is why the marking carries **four digits after the CE**: the number of the body supervising that production.

**Mandatory instructions.** The manufacturer must supply instructions for use, inspection, storage, service life and retirement criteria, in the language of the country. This is not courtesy documentation: it is the reference that determines when you retire an item.

## What this means when buying

**A bare `CE` on climbing gear is a warning sign.** If there are no four digits after it, either it is not category III or something does not add up. Ask before buying.

**Keep the instructions.** This is the most common mistake in clubs: ten harnesses are bought, the box with the leaflet goes in the bin, and three years later nobody knows what service life was declared. Scan them and store them alongside the record for that item. If you do not have them, most manufacturers publish them on their website by model.

**Ask for the EU declaration of conformity** on large purchases. The manufacturer must make it available, usually online. For a club buying gear for its members, having it on file is prudent.

**Be wary of bargains without traceability.** Parallel imports, second-hand without history, marketplaces with no identifiable manufacturer: in category III, not knowing where an item came from is reason enough not to put it into circulation.

## The detail almost nobody checks

The marking also includes the **harmonised standard** it meets: EN 892 for dynamic ropes, EN 12277 for harnesses, EN 12492 for mountaineering helmets, EN 566 for slings, EN 12275 for carabiners.

That matters because **the standard defines the intended use**. An EN 1077 ski helmet is not equivalent to an EN 12492 one: they protect against different impacts. Buying the cheap one because "it is a helmet" is exactly the kind of decision an expert witness would point at afterwards.

## What to do with this in practice

Add two fields to your equipment record that almost nobody has:

- **Standard it meets** — EN 892, EN 12277...
- **Where the instructions are** — a link or a reference to the file

They cost thirty seconds when registering an item and they answer "when does this have to be retired?" for its entire service life. It is the natural companion to the [inspection record](/en/blog/climbing-gear-inspection-club).
