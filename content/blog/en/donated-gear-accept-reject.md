A long-standing member turns up with a bag. Inside there are two ropes, four harnesses and a handful of quickdraws. They are donating it to the club with the best intentions in the world.

It is one of the trickiest moments in club equipment management, and almost no club has written criteria for it.

## The problem is not the gear: it is the history

A donated rope may be in perfect condition. The problem is that you cannot know, because what determines whether an item is still safe is largely **invisible**: hard falls, chemical contact, years stored in a car boot in the sun.

When you buy new gear, its history starts with you. When you receive it as a donation, you start with a gap that can no longer be filled.

And here is the uncomfortable part: if that rope fails, the club will not be able to argue that it did not know. At best it will be able to argue that it did not check.

## Criteria by type of gear

**Safety textile — ropes, harnesses, slings — with no documented history: no.**

That is the hard rule, and it is worth holding. Without a legible date of manufacture, without knowing the use it has had and without the manufacturer's instructions, there is no way to apply the retirement criteria. Accepting it means putting an item into circulation that you will never be able to make an informed decision about.

**Metal — carabiners, belay devices, hangers: case by case.**

Metal allows a far more conclusive visual inspection: deformation, cracking, wear at the running surface, gate function. If it is clean, the marking is legible and it works correctly, it can be assessed. Still not ideal, but defensible.

**Helmets: no.**

A helmet that has taken an impact may show no external damage and have lost its absorption capacity. Without history, no.

**Non-critical gear — rucksacks, sleeping bags, service ropes, anything that does not hold a person: yes, no problem.**

Here donations are welcome and create no risk.

## The exception that does work

Gear donated **with complete documentation**: manufacturer's instructions, dates of manufacture and first use, and a credible usage history. This usually happens when the donor is another club, an activities company or a professional who kept records.

In that case it is not really a blind donation: it is a transfer with traceability, and it can be assessed like any other item.

## How to say no

This is the hardest part, because the donor is acting out of affection for the club and a refusal can feel like an insult.

What works:

**Make the rule the club's, not yours.** "We have a protocol that does not allow textile gear without history" is very different from "I don't trust your ropes". Write the criterion down, get it approved by the committee and publish it. That way you stop deciding case by case and stop being the one saying no.

**Offer a dignified destination.** Donated gear that does not enter the safety inventory can serve for knot practice, training, fixed work lines or display. Saying "this can't be lent out, but it's perfect for our beginners' workshops" preserves the gesture and removes the risk.

**Explain the actual reason.** Most people understand the history argument perfectly once you explain it. What they do not understand is a "no" without a reason.

## Document the donation, accept it or not

Even if you turn the gear down, note that it arrived, who donated it, what it was and what was decided. It costs a minute and stops the same bag reappearing two years later with nobody remembering why it was set aside.

And if you accept it, it enters the inventory like any other item: with its [own identifier and full record](/en/blog/club-equipment-inventory-from-scratch), and the provenance field marked as a donation.
