Identifying every item is the prerequisite for any inventory. The question is with what, and there is a fair amount of misplaced technological enthusiasm here.

I have tried all three options on club gear. This is what holds up.

## Printed QR: the one that usually wins

**Cost:** cents per label.
**Reading:** any phone, no app.
**Durability:** good if you choose the right substrate.

For most clubs, a QR on a polyester or laminated vinyl label solves the problem. You print it at home, anyone reads it with their camera, and it depends on no special hardware.

Its real limits: it scratches under heavy abrasion, and it does not read well with mud, gloves or poor light — which are exactly the conditions mountain gear comes back in.

**A trick that works:** print the code as text underneath the QR too. The day the QR will not scan, somebody can type `CLB-C-014` by hand. It sounds obvious and almost nobody does it.

## NFC: better experience, more friction than it looks

**Cost:** between €0.30 and €1 per tag, depending on format.
**Reading:** hold the phone near it, no aiming or focusing.
**Durability:** excellent in encapsulated form.

NFC is clearly superior in real use: it works with mud, in low light and with one hand occupied. For group returns — fifteen people handing gear back at once — the difference is noticeable.

Two problems worth knowing before you buy:

**Metal interferes.** A standard NFC tag stuck on a carabiner or an anchor simply will not read. *On-metal* tags with a ferrite layer exist and work, but they cost more and are thicker.

**Not all phones cooperate equally.** On Android it is seamless. On iPhone it works with well-formed NDEF tags, but the experience depends on the version and sometimes requires opening the app. If your club has a mix of devices, test with two or three real phones before buying a hundred tags.

## Engraving or permanent marking: for what will not take a sticker

On heavily used metal hardware, labels eventually come off. That is where laser engraving or low-impact stamping is the durable option.

A warning: **nothing is engraved or drilled on textile gear or helmets**. On textile, marking is done with a pen approved by the manufacturer, in the area the manufacturer specifies. An industrial marker or a solvent can degrade the fibres without leaving a visible trace.

## What I would do

A normal club, starting out:

| Gear | Identification |
|---|---|
| Ropes and slings | Heat-shrink with printed code + text |
| Harnesses | Sewn textile label or approved marking |
| Helmets | Vinyl sticker on the inner surface |
| Metal | Engraving or on-metal tag |

And the QR or NFC **on top of that identification**, not instead of it. The human-readable identifier is the one that survives a label falling off, a flat battery or a change of system.

## The underlying mistake

The temptation is to pick the technology first and then work out what to do with it. The useful order is the reverse: if your problem is not knowing what gear you have, cheap QR solves it today. If your problem is that group returns are a twenty-minute mess, NFC pays for itself.

And if you do not have the inventory set up yet, [start there](/en/blog/club-equipment-inventory-from-scratch): tagging gear that is not recorded anywhere achieves nothing.
